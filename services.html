01 · Context & problem

Requests travelled further than the work did.

With no shared record, ownership, urgency and history lived in people’s heads.

The organisation runs several schools, a college, academies and grounds, coordinated by a central administration in another block. Maintenance concerns were written on lists, phoned in or messaged, and then relayed onward.

Before: one maintenance request

The missing piece was a reliable operational record.Reports were incomplete, ownership was hard to follow, pending work was invisible and management had little view of workload or performance.

Problem statement: how might we build a maintenance platform so simple that a user who struggles with smartphone login can raise a ticket in under two minutes, while giving operations teams the ownership, tracking and analytics they need — all without buying a complex enterprise tool?

03 · Discovery & principles

Not a ticket form. A chain of ownership.

Make ownership explicit, keep administration apart from execution, and leave a trail.

Discovery started by shadowing the existing process end to end. The team then met every stakeholder group — Central Admin, Space Admins, supervisors and maintainers — to understand their current pain points and anticipate the challenges of moving them onto a digital system. Each group was onboarded in under five minutes.

Why build, not buy?Every existing tool evaluated was too complex for the user base. For many users, simply logging into an application was a point of confusion. The team needed something with no unnecessary complexity, only the features and data required for operations and analytics. It also needed to be a web app — mobile-first, compatible with any OS, any browser and outdated smartphones — and designed to integrate into a larger in-house ERP system later. No off-the-shelf product met all three constraints.
The design went through multiple rounds because the users were not smartphone-fluent.The core constraint: reporting must take only a few taps. Users needed to raise an issue, view it and track progress without reading a manual. This drove a repeated cycle of simplification — removing fields, collapsing navigation depth and making status visible at a glance. Each round started with real users, not assumptions.

Explicit ownership

Every ticket has one accountable supervisor, and Central Admin can reassign.

Administer apart from execute

Maintainers see only their tasks and selected ticket context, not full ticket controls.

Review where it matters

Reviewers are optional, and can be required before work starts.

Evidence and history

Notes, images, optional voice and activity log stay with the ticket.

Digital beside the phone

Urgent issues can still be called in. The ticket becomes the record.

Few taps, not fewer features

Reporting, viewing and tracking were each reduced to the minimum number of screens after repeated redesigns with real users.

Web app, any device

Mobile-first and browser-based so it works on any OS, any screen size and outdated smartphones — no app store, no installs.

Built to integrate

Designed from the start to plug into a larger in-house ERP system, so the data model and access control were scoped with that future in mind.

05 · Challenges & resolutions

Problems we didn't anticipate.

Most were caught early through iterative prototype testing. A few only appeared at scale.

Multiple submissions on slow networks

Users on slow connections clicked submit multiple times, creating duplicate tickets. Fixed by disabling the submit button immediately on first click and showing a loading label until the form confirmed submission. Caught post-launch through user feedback.

Image loading speed

Tickets with photo attachments loaded slowly, especially on older devices. All images uploaded to the system are now compressed and converted to WebP format, significantly reducing file size and load time without visible quality loss.

Supervisors continuing by phone

Some supervisors kept using phone calls instead of the system because they needed to understand the issue before visiting the site, and the app had no way to contact the reporter directly. Adding in-app dialling — one tap from the ticket — resolved this. Traditional communication is now integrated with the ticket record rather than running parallel to it.

Users who cannot read or type

Some users found text-based reporting a barrier. Voice note attachments were added so a user can record the problem in their own words, the same way they would on a messaging app. This removed the last significant friction point for non-tech-fluent reporters.

Prototype testing before each ship

The team followed an iterative model, testing a prototype extensively before shipping each feature. Most issues — wrong button placement, too much information on one screen, confusing flows — were caught and resolved before reaching users.

06 · Scope & trade-offs

A small team, a tight core, deliberate choices.

Ship ownership and tasks first, then grow from real use.

Initial six-month scopeTicket management, authentication and roles, supervisor assignment, work tasks
Added after launchPerformance reports, site visits, external task sharing, push notifications
Expected to move elsewherePurchase requests and shopping lists, planned for a separate product
Supervisor owns the ticket, not the task

Clear accountability and easy delegation. The cost is that a supervisor receiving a delegated task does not gain maintainer-level permissions, which keeps responsibility from blurring.

Optional review over mandatory review

Avoids slowing routine work. The cost is that quality checks depend on Central Admin choosing when review is required.

Server-rendered Django with HTMX over an API-first single-page app

Fast for a two-person team to build and run. The cost is no public REST API, which keeps the product focused on its own web workflow.

General-user reporting built, not activated

The capability exists for scaling, but the live workflow stays with admins who can raise complete tickets. The cost is that front-line staff still depend on their space’s admin.

Keep purchasing out of the core

Purchase requests and shopping lists were added, then earmarked for migration so the maintenance product does not become a procurement tool.

07 · How it was built

Reliable before elegant.

A Django monolith with role-aware views, tenant-scoped access and tested workflows.

Architecture

Django 5.2 with PostgreSQL, server-rendered templates, Bootstrap 5 and HTMX. Docker deployment with health checks.

Security

Organisation-scoped access, hashed passwords and PINs, CSRF protection and expiring external links.

Media

Images resized up to 1920 px, converted to WebP and stored with randomised names in object storage.

Notifications

Firebase push with token validation and cleanup, in batches of up to 500 recipients.

Quality

Tests cover sign-in flows, role permissions, tenant boundaries, ticket and task workflows, reports and media.

09 · Learnings & limits

What we took away.

Ownership, permissions and designing for the least comfortable user were harder than the ticket form.

Ownership is the product

A named owner at every stage did more than any extra field or screen.

Permissions are UX

Splitting ticket administration from task execution kept screens simple and responsibility clear.

Design for the least comfortable user

The design had to be reworked multiple times after testing with users who were not smartphone-fluent. Each round removed steps that seemed obvious to us and invisible to them. The result was a flow that worked for everyone — not just the technically confident.

Scope discipline matters

Moving purchasing out of the core protected the maintenance workflow.

Plan measurement early

No pre-launch data was collected on the old process. Setting up a baseline study at week one would have made the impact immediately provable.

Traditional methods don't disappear — integrate them

Supervisors kept calling because calls were faster for understanding. The right response was not to remove calls but to put them inside the system. Integrating familiar behaviour was faster than changing it.

Ship iteratively, test every prototype

Most issues were found before users ever saw them. The ones that slipped through — duplicate submissions, slow image loads — were caught quickly because the feedback loop post-launch was fast.

Known limitations

Urgent issues still travel by phone in parallel — the ticket becomes the record after the fact. General-user reporting is built but not yet active. No before-state data was kept, so impact can be described but not quantified against the old process. Performance ratings reflect configured factors, not a complete measure of staff quality.

09 · What’s next

From what it does to what it changes.

Five next bets, starting with proof.

Reconstruct the baseline

Run a staff survey to estimate pre-launch request volumes and typical reply times. Even rough numbers would let the 48-hour figure tell a before-and-after story.

Target durations

Define expected times by ticket and task type.

Adoption by role and space

Count meaningful actions, not logins.

Trend reporting

Backlog, reopened and overdue work over time.

Notification effectiveness

Measure first-action delay after an alert.

ERP integration

Connect the ticketing data into the broader in-house ERP system as the next phase of the platform.

New features in progress

Multiple user suggestions are actively being worked on based on post-launch feedback.
